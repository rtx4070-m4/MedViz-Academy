📊 Visual
Which visual reveals it?

Compare categories

Use a bar chart for discrete healthcare groups: departments, wards, treatment arms, age groups.

Trend over time

Use a line chart when observations have an ordered time variable: daily cases, monthly admissions, annual mortality.

Part-to-whole

Use pie/donut for a small number of meaningful categories. If there are many categories, prefer a bar chart.

Relationship

Use a scatter plot to examine two numeric variables, such as waiting time and satisfaction score.

Many categories

Use packed bubbles when magnitude across many categories is the message, but avoid it when exact comparison is essential.

Hierarchy

Use a treemap for nested composition: hospital → department → unit, or disease → subtype → diagnosis.

Patterns across two dimensions

Use a heat map for matrices such as weekday × hour, ward × month, or symptom × age group.

Geographic distribution

Use maps when location is analytically meaningful. Choropleths show area-level rates; proportional symbols show point magnitude.

📚 Every Concept in the Reference

Click a concept to understand when it works in healthcare analytics.
1
Why context matters

A number without its denominator, time period, population or comparison can mislead. “Mortality increased” is incomplete without knowing from what baseline and among whom.

2
Bar chart — compare categories

Best for comparing discrete groups. Sort bars when ranking is important and keep scales honest.

3
Line chart — trends over time

Best for continuous/ordered time. Match the time granularity to the clinical question: day, week, month, quarter or year.

4
Pie / donut — composition

Shows how a whole is divided. Keep categories few and mutually exclusive; a donut is useful when a central KPI helps.

5
Scatter plot — relationships

Shows whether two numeric variables move together. Look for direction, strength, clusters and outliers; correlation does not prove causation.

6
Packed bubbles — many categories

Bubble area communicates magnitude across many groups. Good for visual scanning; weaker than bars for precise comparison.

7
Treemap — hierarchical composition

Nested rectangles show parent-child structure and relative magnitude. Useful for hospital service lines and disease classifications.

8
Maps — geographic distribution

Use proportional symbols for point-level quantities and choropleths for region-level rates or proportions. Do not use geography when location adds no meaning.

9
Heat map — patterns across two dimensions

Color intensity makes high/low combinations easy to spot, such as ED arrivals by weekday and hour.

10
Golden rule

Don't ask “Which chart looks best?” Ask “Which chart makes the answer easiest to see?” Right visual → clear pattern → better insight → better decision.

⚠️ Common Visualization Mistakes

❌ Pie charts for too many categories

Switch to a sorted bar chart when slices become hard to compare.

❌ 3D charts that distort perception

Use flat 2D marks so visual area maps honestly to data.

❌ Too many colors without meaning

Reserve color for an analytical variable or meaningful alert state.

❌ Not sorting categories

Sort bars when ranking or magnitude is the message.

❌ Misleading axes or scales

Especially dangerous for clinical rates and outcome comparisons.

❌ Overloading dashboards

Give each visual a clear question and prioritize the decision that matters.

❌ Confusing association with causation

A scatter plot can reveal association; causal inference needs study design and appropriate analysis.

❌ Ignoring denominators

A count of 50 means something different in a population of 500 versus 500,000.
